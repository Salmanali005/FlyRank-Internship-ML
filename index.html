Ranking Pages for Review: A Baseline Rule vs Simple Models on Search Data

Lane 2: Refresh / Content Opportunity Scoring. FlyRank ML Internship capstone. Decision-support research, observational data only.

Abstract. Content teams cannot review every page, so they need a short list of pages to look at first. I asked whether a simple model can rank pages by decline risk better than a transparent rule built on staleness and visibility. I used a 30,000-page anonymized starter sample, a proxy label for decline, and a client-held-out split where the test clients were never seen in training. The Week 4 baseline rule scored a ROC AUC of 0.475, while logistic regression and gradient boosting both scored about 0.61, and the top 100 pages ranked by gradient boosting declined 73% of the time against a 51% base rate. The lift is modest, it rests on a proxy label, and it supports review prioritization only, not any claim about causes.

Introduction

A reviewer who checks pages in alphabetical order wastes time on healthy pages and may miss pages that are losing visibility. The decision this work supports is simple: which pages should a reviewer open first? A wrong call costs something in two ways. A false alarm spends limited review time on a page that did not need it, and a miss lets a declining page keep losing traffic unnoticed. Because reviewers only read the top of a list, I judge the work by the quality of the top of the ranking, not by overall accuracy.

Data

I used the anonymized starter dataset from the FlyRank ML Internship: 30,000 pages, one row per page, with 90-day search and engagement signals. All pages have impressions and are at least 90 days old. Identifiers are pseudonymized and used only for grouping. I excluded FlyRank product scores and flags, the label field trend_direction and its companion trend_pct, and every last_30d and prev_30d column. No client names, domains, URLs, or queries appear anywhere in this paper.

Methodology

Unit and label. One row is one page. The label is the proxy trend_direction == "down". It is a bucket computed from the current window, not a future outcome, so it is a beginner proxy for the future decline I actually care about. The test set has 51.1% declining pages (training: 55.0%).

Features. Log impressions, log clicks, average position, log word count, log days since last update, and one-hot content type. Missing word counts are filled with 0. All are known before the prediction moment.

Baseline. A transparent score that multiplies the percentile rank of days since update by the percentile rank of impressions, written before any model was fit.

Models. Logistic regression, a depth-5 decision tree, a random forest, and gradient boosting. I did not reward complexity: a more complex model had to beat simpler ones on the same split.

Validation. A client-held-out split puts 25 clients (23,837 pages) in training and 7 clients (6,163 pages) in testing, with zero clients in both. I also ran a grouped 5-fold check. The baseline and all models use the same split and metrics.

Leakage checks. I added a column copied from the label on purpose. The ROC AUC jumped to 1.0 and fell to 0.723 once the column was removed, which is why I treat any too-good score with suspicion. The final feature set contains no label-derived column, future window, or product flag.

Every model beats the baseline, but the gains are modest. The baseline falls slightly below 0.5 on this test set, so staleness and visibility alone carried no usable ranking signal here. Logistic regression and gradient boosting tie on ROC AUC (0.610 vs 0.611), so the simple model matches the complex one and complexity earned nothing. The grouped 5-fold spread is wide for the baseline, so I treat all differences as directional.

Interpretation. The model leans on impressions (0.070), clicks (0.064), and average position (0.031). Word count adds almost nothing (0.003), and days since update is slightly negative (-0.013), which matches the mixed staleness result from my signal audit. This shows which signals the model used, not that any signal causes decline.

Errors. Flagging the top 20% of test pages by predicted risk caught 770 declining pages and produced 466 false alarms, while 2,379 declining pages were missed. False alarms are high-visibility pages (median 1,156 impressions, median position 15.4) that did not decline. Missed pages are lower-traffic (median 309 impressions), so the model is better at flagging visible pages than quiet ones.

Limitations

  • The label is a proxy from the current window. The features cover the same 90 days that the label compares, so part of the model's skill may come from overlapping measurements, not from predicting a future decline.
  • This is a 30,000-page starter sample with 32 clients. It is not a benchmark on the full warehouse, and results must be earned again there with a future-window label and a time-aware split.
  • The test uses only 7 clients, so the numbers could move with a different split.
  • Staleness had almost no data in its oldest buckets (only 174 pages are 180+ days old), so the baseline rule fired rarely.
  • The work is observational. It cannot show that refreshing a page causes recovery, it makes no claim about Google ranking factors, and it says nothing about AI search visibility.

Ranked recommendations

Used as decision-support, the ranked queue gives reviewers a place to start. Ranked by gradient boosting on the held-out clients, the top 20 pages declined 80% of the time and the top 100 declined 73%, against a 51% base rate. Of the top 100, 66 carry the reason code visible_decline_risk (500+ impressions and in the top 20% of risk) and 34 carry model_decline_risk.

  • Review first: pages with visible_decline_risk, which combine real demand with a high risk score. A reviewer checks whether the drop is real, seasonal, or absorbed by a related page before editing.
  • Monitor: pages with model_decline_risk and low impressions. They are likely to be declining but have little traffic at stake.
  • Human check before acting: a recommendation is wrong if the page is stable, seasonal, or if a sibling page took its demand.
